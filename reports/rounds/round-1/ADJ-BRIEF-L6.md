Rules:

1. **Rule on each finding with exactly one of UPHELD / NARROWED / OVERTURNED**, and say in one sentence
   what survives. NARROWED is the most common correct answer and the most under-used: an attacker is
   often right about a defect and wrong about its scope.
2. **A PRESCRIBED FIX IS NOT ADJUDICATED JUST BECAUSE THE DIAGNOSIS WAS RIGHT.** Rule separately on the
   diagnosis and on the proposed correction. A correction that installs a STRONGER false claim has been
   recorded **six times** in this project: one would have put Michigan into a variable named "oldest"
   against Massachusetts; another would have destroyed the only external validation a reconstruction
   had. If the diagnosis holds and the fix does not, say exactly that -- UPHELD on the defect,
   OVERTURNED on the repair.

   **And when you DO endorse a fix, say what it costs.** A fix you endorse that changes a few
   characters and needs no re-measurement is FREE, and saying so is what lets the round record it as
   owed rather than as an opinion: write "the fix is free" (or name its cost) in the ruling, so the
   runner can mark the item `{"repair": {"free": true, "endorsed": "<this ruling>"}}`. An endorsed
   free repair that nobody applies is this protocol's most repeated waste -- round 46's completeness
   critic found six of them reaching neither the document nor the acceptance record, and round 47
   inherited three more, applied after its parent closed and seen by no adversary.
3. **Verify the WHOLE SET, never the one input the attacker chose.** D4's excise overlay was right *by

   NEW error. Before endorsing a fix to one element, check what it does to the others.
4. **Test the attack's PREMISE.** The most striking refutations of recent rounds were algebraically
   correct and rested on a premise nobody checked. Re-run it: the database is live and the scripts run.
5. **Check what earlier rounds already ruled**, and say so where it bears:

       scripts/round history "<subject>" --kind adjudication

   An attacker re-filing a settled point is filing a WEAKER finding, and saying so is part of your job.
6. **Our own method cards are the standard for a statistical dispute** -- `refs/methods/` (each card
   written because an analysis was RETRACTED). Across round 35 all five adjudicators cited ZERO of
   them. If the attack and the claim disagree about a method, the card governing it decides, and if no
   card governs it, say that.
7. **An OVERTURNED finding must say what the attacker should have checked instead.** An overturn that
   only says "wrong" teaches the next round nothing.
8. **Record a correct minority.** If you overturn a point that more than one attacker made, or uphold
   one that only a single attacker saw, say so explicitly -- agreement among agents of one model family
   is not corroboration, and this is the only place a lone correct critic gets recorded.
9. **END WITH A VERDICT TABLE, and give every finding a row.** This exact shape, because it is the one
   thing about an adjudication that is read mechanically:

       | finding | verdict | why |
       |---|---|---|
       | F1 | UPHELD | the date is checkable and it is wrong |
       | F2 | NARROWED | the defect is real; the prescribed fix is not |

   One row per finding id the attack filed, and the id in the FIRST cell. Prose rulings are welcome
   above it -- write as much as the finding needs -- but the table is what makes the round countable.
   Measured over rounds 34-42: adjudications that ended with a table were read mechanically every time,
   and the 6.9% that still cannot be read are the ones that restructured the attacker's ids away (roman
   numerals, thematic sections) so that no rule can pair a ruling with what it ruled on.

   **A COMPOUND verdict needs a table row too.** `**NARROWED on its wording leg; OVERTURNED on its
   causal leg**` is a legitimate and common ruling, and it is exactly what a reader cannot flatten:
   put the verdict that governs the finding AS A WHOLE in the table, and keep the legs in the prose.
